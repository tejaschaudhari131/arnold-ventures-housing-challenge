## How to read a large percent

The primary outcome is units, not the percent lift. Pennsylvania's ADU pathway is 46,261 completions over five years, about 9,250 a year, on a stock of 5.78 million homes: roughly 0.16 percent of stock per year. The 42.5 percent lift looks large because the recent permit run-rate is small relative to that stock. Rhode Island's 43.9 percent ADU lift is 2,821 homes. Rank reforms by units inside one state. Do not rank states by the percent column.

The fee cut is the most stable reform: about 5 to 7 percent of baseline completions in ten states, and 3.4 percent in Hawaii, where a $25,000 saving is a smaller share of a high cost base. Parking is smaller still, 1 to 4 percent, because the market still builds most of the spaces the mandate required. Transit-oriented density is local. It is the third-largest reform in Connecticut (2,514) and material in Virginia (10,789, mostly Northern Virginia prices against a low multifamily cost index) and Oregon (3,164). It is 8 homes in New Hampshire, which has no local fixed guideway under the definition used here, and 56 in Wisconsin. Hawaii's podium barely pencils: the multifamily cost index is 2.25.

## What the ranking is actually saying

ADUs lead where a large single-family stock is not already entitled and the homeowner yield clears an $250-per-square-foot prototype (Pennsylvania, Wisconsin, Arizona, Tennessee, Rhode Island, New Hampshire). Missing middle leads where prices are high enough for a conversion tail and the legal gap is still open (Connecticut, Utah, Oregon, Hawaii, Virginia). Teardowns of a sound house generally do not clear. The missing-middle units are additions that keep the house, and in several states the five-year absorption cap binds before the zoning envelope does.

The package is larger than the sum of the five standalone reforms in every state in this central case. Pennsylvania's five reforms sum to 71,273 and the package is 74,114. The extra units are fee and parking savings applied inside the zoning pro formas. Overlap still removes double-counted parcels: in Connecticut the package's missing-middle piece is 3,503 against 4,813 standalone, and its transit piece is 1,909 against 2,514. The parts of the package add to the package total. They are not a second estimate.

## What moves the number

One-at-a-time swings on the combined package, low versus high of each published slider:

- Pennsylvania (central 74,114): ADU adoption hazard (swing 65,471), price level (54,563), construction-cost index (45,504), conversion cost (40,415).
- Virginia (central 105,804): conversion cost (51,486), price level (51,010), construction cost (45,622), middle-housing pace (41,159).
- Connecticut (central 11,033): conversion cost (8,490), price level (7,922), margin dispersion (6,675), construction cost (6,182).
- Hawaii (central 8,958): conversion cost (8,755), price level (8,695), construction cost (8,099).

The adoption hazard and the price-to-cost gap dominate. Lot-size microdata does not. Hiding Connecticut's observed lot bins (feasibility 96 percent versus the 89 percent imputation) changes ADU completions by −7.2 percent and the package by −2.0 percent. Missing middle, transit, parking, and fees do not use the lot bins, so they do not move. Collapsing Connecticut to one state-median screen, which is what a team does when it skips county ACS, cuts missing middle by 68 percent and transit-oriented density by 86 percent. Fees barely move (−0.3 to +0.7 percent on the tier-1 states) because that channel is linear in permits and county permits already sum to the state.

The seeded Monte Carlo band on the package widens when parcel fields are hidden in Connecticut (1.07×) and Wisconsin (1.26×). Utah is essentially unchanged (1.01×). Hawaii's band is 0.93× the full band: the incomplete-data screen also drops the State Land Use wall and treats the whole single-family stock as eligible, which shifts the point estimate up by 6.7 percent and does not widen every draw. Uncertainty is not a single multiplier. The app reports the band next to the point.

## What this comparison cannot support

It cannot say which statute text to pass, which parcel will redevelop, or what existing homes will sell for after the reform. There is no equilibrium price feedback inside the five years beyond the absorption cap. Already-legal shares are assumptions, widest in Connecticut (ADU residual only) and Oregon (urban-growth-boundary assumption, not a measured layer). Station counts are a screened inventory with a scale slider, not a walk from each parcel to a GTFS stop. The $50,000 fee stack is the challenge's comparison case, not a measured fee schedule. A state whose real fees are lower will see a smaller scenario-5 effect; the slider is there for that conversation.
